Frame up front: this talk is not "use AI for genomics." It's about what you point the AI at. The GTN is 536 human-written, human-reviewed tutorials — and it happens to be exactly the corpus an agent needs. Humans write it; machines read it. That asymmetry is the whole talk.

The Bottleneck Isn't Sequencing Anymore

Sequencing has reached labs where analysis expertise hasn't followed.

When a new pathogen dataset lands, two bad options
  • Wait — for a workshop, a course, a collaborator with free time
  • Ask an AI — which will cheerfully produce a plausible pipeline
What "plausible" costs you

Tool versions that never existed together. A filtering step quietly dropped — the one that decides whether two isolates look like transmission. And no record of either.

The framing for this talk

An agent with no curriculum improvises. The GTN is a curriculum.

Motivation
Say out loud that the room contains both AI enthusiasts and AI skeptics, and that this talk is aimed at both. The failure mode isn't that the model refuses — it's that it answers confidently. The concrete example to speak, not read: an invented TB pipeline that skips the low-coverage/repeat-region masking step produces transmission clusters that look fine and are wrong.

The GTN
Lead with the One Health list, not the numbers — this audience recognizes those titles. TB transmission clustering, AMR gene identification, pox virus tiled-amplicon: these are ASTMH problems, already written up, already with workflows. Then the numbers for scale. Note the fourth bullet: a Nextflow pipeline gives you the steps; a GTN tutorial tells you why the steps. If asked about the name: "One Health" is the WHO/FAO/WOAH/UNEP framing, not a Galaxy coinage — human, animal and ecosystem health as one interlinked system. And it's a different kind of topic than the rest of the GTN: internally it's a synthetic, tag-based topic, with no directory of its own. Nothing was written for it. It's a re-indexing of tutorials that already lived under variant-analysis, evolution, genome-annotation and computational-chemistry, pulled together by what you're trying to find out instead of by technique. Helena Rasche built the mechanism in May 2023 and handed the curation to Wolfgang Maier, Peter van Heusden and Tomas Klingström — Galaxy variant analysis, SANBI, and SLU veterinary informatics. Human, animal, environment, staffed that way. That's this talk's argument in miniature: the material was already there, it just needed to be findable. (Count check: the topic carries 9 materials today; the card shows 7 — a third SARS-CoV-2 tutorial and a molecular-docking one are left off on purpose.)

What this means in practice

No scraping. No RAG pipeline to build. The curriculum is already a corpus.

The GTN
This is the pivot of the talk. Nobody designed the GTN as AI infrastructure — it's Markdown, YAML and JSON because that's how you maintain 536 tutorials with 700 volunteers across a dozen countries. The accident is that those are exactly the formats an agent ingests without help. Live-demo option: run that curl and show the JSON.

The GTN Has Rules for Agents — In the Repo

Agents may not
  • Write tutorials, news, events, or learning pathways — any learner-facing prose
  • Write your pull request descriptions, issues, or replies to reviewers
Agents may
  • Answer, analyze, distill, check, suggest, review
  • Assist on the codebase — with disclosure, and an assisted-by: commit trailer
Why — and it's a scientific argument

Most GTN contributors are not native English speakers. The community would rather have your imperfect words than a model's smooth ones — homogenized prose flattens a deliberately multi-voice curriculum, and reviewing it shifts the work onto volunteers.

How
All three are available now and none require you to pick a model. Galaxy MCP works with any MCP client — Claude Code, Codex, your own script, a local model on university HPC. The difference isn’t the pipeline

One maps to H37Rv because that is what everyone says. The other maps to the ancestor genome because that is what the tutorial does — and starts you two tutorials earlier.

Walkthrough
Both halves are real Orbit output, same question, captured 2026-11-17. LEFT is Orbit asked cold: a textbook core-SNP pipeline, confident and basically correct, no source. RIGHT is the same question after the GTN functions were wired up to resolve the prerequisite graph. Three beats worth landing, in order. ONE: it returns a path, not a link — collections first, because 48 isolates is a collections problem before it is a phylogenetics problem, and that is the half of the question the cold answer ignored. TWO: H37Rv versus the ancestor genome. The cold answer gives the textbook reference; the GTN tutorial actually uses the inferred most-recent-common-ancestor genome of the MTB complex, which differs from H37Rv by SNPs inserted to model the ancestor of all lineages. That is a better choice for transmission work and you do not get it from general knowledge — you get it from a person who wrote it down. THREE: the index-case caveat is not in the tutorial metadata at all; it is prose inside a question-and-answer box in the tutorial body. The agent read the page. Honest framing if asked: this took three rounds of adding functionality, and it still does not surface the importable CI-tested workflow IDs or ask whether you are starting from FASTQs or VCFs. The curation was already there. The plumbing to reach it is what we had to build.

Why a Tutorial Beats a Prompt

Four things a GTN tutorial ships that a confident paragraph doesn't.

Named input data
Pinned to a Zenodo record — you can start from the same bytes
A workflow that has run
140 of them run under CI against a real Galaxy server
Expected outputs
Something to diff your result against before you trust it
A stated rationale
Why this step, this threshold, this reference — in a human's words
The version for the skeptic in row three

You don't have to trust the agent. You can check its homework against a page anyone can read.

Why it works
Payoff slide. The fourth card is the one that can't be automated and is the reason the GTN bans AI-written tutorials — a stated rationale is only worth something if a person actually held the opinion. Tie back: Galaxy records what ran, the GTN records why it should have.

What This Does Not Fix

Wrong tutorial, confidently
Topic match is not study-design match. The agent can't know your sampling frame.
Teaching data is small on purpose
Subsampled so a workshop finishes. Your 500 isolates are not the demo set.
228 workflows still untested
"Has a workflow" is not yet the same as "is verified."
  • No GTN MCP server yet — today an agent reads the JSON API and the Markdown directly. An obvious next step, not a shipped one.
  • The GTN states plainly that it cannot offer support on anything referencing AI output — grounding doesn't transfer responsibility.
Fair enough

These are the questions to ask of any AI-assisted result — including the ones in this talk.

Limits
Do not skip this slide for time. In a room with real skeptics, naming the limits yourself is what makes the rest credible. The subsampled-data point is the most practically important one — it is the single most common way a tutorial-derived analysis goes wrong at real scale.

Why the gap matters more than it used to

A hole in the curriculum used to mean no workshop for your pathogen. Now it also means an agent grounding on the GTN finds nothing to ground on — and improvises instead.

The gap
This number is checkable and you should invite them to check it: every topic from the GTN JSON API, 629 unique materials, searching titles and tags. TB is genuinely excellent — three tutorials and real depth, because a community decided to build it. Nothing structural stopped anyone writing the malaria equivalent; nobody has. Then land the closing line, which is the hinge of the whole talk: grounding only protects you where the curriculum exists, so the coverage gap is now also an AI-reliability gap for exactly the diseases in this room.
